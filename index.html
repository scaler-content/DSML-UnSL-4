Opening story

1One message for two million customers

👩‍💼 Priya · Head of Marketing

FreshKart delivers groceries in 15 minutes from neighbourhood dark stores. Every Monday, the company sends the exact same push notification to all two million customers: "Flat 20% off today."

"Opens are falling, unsubscribes are rising, and our discount budget is leaking. Some people would have bought anyway. Others haven't opened the app in months, and a discount won't bring them back. Our customers are not all the same — can you tell me which groups we actually have?"

Arjun, three months into FreshKart's data team, has a problem: nobody has ever tagged a customer as a "bargain hunter" or a "premium shopper." There is no label column, so a classifier is out of the question.

Then he remembers how FreshKart decides where to open a new dark store: mark where customers live, drop a few candidate pins on the map, assign every customer to the nearest pin, then slide each pin to the centre of the customers it serves. Repeat until the pins stop moving.

That's a clustering algorithm. FreshKart has been running K-Means on a map for years — Arjun just needs to run it on customer behaviour instead.

Core concept

2What is clustering?

Clustering asks an algorithm to look at unlabelled data and make groups itself — no target column required.

Definition

Clustering divides data points into groups (clusters) so that points in the same group are more similar to each other than to points in other groups. Points in the same cluster should be close; points in different clusters should be far apart.

What does "similar" mean?

Computers don't have a feeling for similarity — it has to be defined as closeness, measured with a distance. The most common choice is Euclidean distance between two customers x and y across d features:

dist(x, y) = √ Σj=1…d (xj − yj)²

K-Means specifically works with the squared Euclidean distance. Click the two points below to see how distance is computed.

Why this fits Priya's problem

There is no target column in FreshKart's customer data, and the goal is simply to find groups of similar customers. That is exactly what clustering is for.

Check yourself

Arjun's data has columns like clicks, visits and spend, with no column saying what type each customer is. Why can't he use classification, and what should he use instead?

Classification needs known classes to train on — here nobody has tagged any customer, so there's nothing to supervise. Clustering groups customers using only their features. Regression predicts a numeric target (a different task), and unsupervised methods exist for exactly this unlabeled situation.

Analogy

3K-Means intuition: placing dark stores

K-Means is just the dark-store planning process, generalised:

  1. Drop K pins (candidate stores) on the map.
  2. Every customer is served by the nearest pin.
  3. Move each pin to the centre of the customers it serves (their average location).
  4. Customers' nearest pin may have changed — repeat steps 2 and 3 until the pins stop moving.

Replace "map location" with "customer behaviour" and "pin" with centroid, and that's K-Means.

K
The number of clusters. You choose it before running the algorithm — it's a hyperparameter.
Centroid
The centre of a cluster, calculated as the average of its points.
Centroid-based clustering
Every point belongs to the cluster of its nearest centroid.

The hard part is finding good centroids — that's what the algorithm in the next section does.

The method

4Lloyd's algorithm: four steps

K-Means is solved in practice with Lloyd's algorithm. Step through it below.

Check yourself

A cluster currently contains the three points (1,2), (3,4) and (5,0). (a) What is its centroid after the update step? (b) If the old centroid was (2,2), has the centroid moved?

The centroid is the average of the points: x = (1+3+5)/3 = 3, y = (2+4+0)/3 = 2 → (3, 2). The old centroid (2,2) is different, so it moved, and the algorithm continues with a new assignment step.

Watching the cost fall

What to notice

The cost never goes up — each assignment step and each update step can only reduce it, so the algorithm is guaranteed to converge. K-Means stops when centroids no longer move, which happens at the same moment the cost stops falling.

Does the starting position matter?

Most starts reach the same low cost (693.8), but a few get stuck in a noticeably worse local solution (around 3,350). Lloyd's algorithm only guarantees a local minimum — which is why sklearn's KMeans runs it several times (n_init) and keeps the best result.

The math underneath

5What K-Means is really minimising

K-Means searches for centroids and clusters that make every point as close as possible to its own cluster's centre:

min Σj=1…K Σxi∈Cj ‖xi − μj‖²

This total is called the Within-Cluster Sum of Squares (WCSS) — inertia in sklearn. In plain terms: take the squared distance from every customer to the centre of its group, add it up within each group, then add that across all groups. Smaller is tighter.

If you know the centroids, the clusters follow automatically — each point simply goes to its nearest centroid, the same dark-store rule as before.

Finding the best assignment of every point to a cluster is a combinatorial, NP-hard problem — the exact solution takes exponentially longer as data grows. Lloyd's algorithm is an approximation: it doesn't guarantee the best answer, but it always improves the objective and works well in practice.

Yes — the cost never goes up, and there are only finitely many ways to assign points, so it must stop. But it stops at a local minimum, sometimes the global one and sometimes a worse one, depending on the starting centroids.

Back to FreshKart

6Preparing the real customer data

2,500 customers, each with an ID and six behavioural features: clicks, visits, amount spent, amount discounted, days since registration, and profile completeness.

2,500 customers 6 behavioural features No target column

Step 1 — drop the identifier

ID is just a name tag. A distance between two ID numbers ("customer 17 is far from customer 5000") is meaningless, and including it would add pure noise to every distance calculation. A pairplot of the remaining features hints at some structure, but with six dimensions it can't show the full picture — that's exactly why an algorithm is needed to group in all dimensions at once.

Step 2 — scaling is essential

K-Means is built entirely on distances, which sum squared differences across every feature. A feature with a large numeric range will dominate that sum regardless of whether it actually matters. Before scaling, amount spent alone accounts for 82.3% of total variance — K-Means would effectively cluster on spend alone and ignore everything else.

Share of total variance, before vs. after scaling
MinMaxScaler brings every feature into the [0, 1] range

Caution

MinMaxScaler is sensitive to outliers — one extreme customer stretches the range and squeezes everyone else into a narrow band. StandardScaler (mean 0, standard deviation 1) is the common alternative.

Judging quality without labels

8How good are these clusters?

Evaluation without labels has two parts: business sense (do the groups mean something actionable — domain knowledge decides this) and internal metrics, numbers computed from the data and the clusters alone. A good score on the numbers never guarantees the clusters are useful to the business.

Intra-cluster distance

How tightly a cluster's points are packed. Low is good.

Inter-cluster distance

How far apart two clusters are. High is good.

The sum of squared distances from each point to its own centroid — exactly the K-Means objective. Lower = tighter clusters.

247.25
FreshKart's K=4 clustering — confirmed by computing it by hand and matching sklearn's inertia_ exactly.
Watch out

WCSS always falls as K rises, reaching zero when every customer is their own cluster — which is useless. It can only compare clusterings with the same K; choosing K needs other reasoning entirely.

D = mini≠j d(Ci,Cj) ÷ maxl Δ(Cl)

Numerator: the smallest distance between any two clusters. Denominator: the largest spread inside any single cluster. Higher is better — there's no universal "good" value, so it's mainly used to compare different clusterings of the same data.

0.0325
FreshKart's K=4 clustering, computed on a 3,000-customer subsample. Example reading: a Dunn index of 5 would mean the closest pair of clusters is 5× as far apart as the widest cluster is wide.
s = (b − a) ÷ max(a, b), s ∈ [−1, 1]

a = average distance to other points in the same cluster. b = average distance to the nearest other cluster. Near +1: well inside its own cluster. Near 0: on the boundary. Negative: probably misassigned.

0.211
Average silhouette score across the sample — modest separation. Only 3.6% of customers have a negative silhouette (possibly misassigned).
Check yourself

Arjun runs K-Means with K=4 and gets WCSS=120, then K=8 and gets WCSS=70. His colleague says "K=8 is clearly better, lower WCSS means tighter clusters." What's wrong with that?

More centroids always means points sit closer to some centroid, so WCSS falls as K rises — reaching zero when every customer is their own cluster, which is useless. WCSS can compare clusterings with the same K; choosing K needs other reasoning plus business sense.
Check yourself

A clustering has a Dunn index of 5. Which statement is correct?

The Dunn index is the ratio of smallest inter-cluster distance to largest intra-cluster distance — it says nothing about the number of clusters and is on a different scale from silhouette.

Key takeaways

Clustering
Groups unlabelled points so that points within a group are close and points in different groups are far apart. "Similar" is defined by a distance.
K-Means / Lloyd's algorithm
Places K centroids, assigns every point to its nearest centroid, moves each centroid to the mean of its points, and repeats until nothing changes.
The objective
Minimises WCSS. Lloyd's algorithm never increases it, but only finds a local minimum — the result depends on the start (hence n_init).
Preprocessing matters
Drop identifiers and scale features, since K-Means is entirely distance-based.
Evaluate two ways
Internal metrics (WCSS, Dunn index, silhouette) for geometry, and business sense (maps, profiles, naming) for usefulness.
Choosing K
WCSS always falls as K rises, so it can never choose K by itself — that needs principled methods, covered next.
